# **Week 4 — Claim, Data, Warrant**

_Due: one paragraph analyzing a text's claim, data, and warrant, plus one original paragraph of your own with claim, data, and warrant clearly marked._

**DUE DATE: Sunday, Sept 13th at Midnight

Every argument, whether it's a Supreme Court brief or a bar bet, rests on the same three-part structure. Learning to see it will change how you read, and how you write.

- **Claim** — the arguable statement you want your reader to accept. Not a fact (facts aren't arguable), but a position someone could reasonably push back on.
- **Data** — the fact, example, or evidence you offer as support. This is the part most students already do instinctively: "here's my proof."
- **Warrant** — the connective tissue between the two. It's the assumption that has to be true for your data to actually support your claim. This is the part almost everyone skips, because it usually feels too obvious to state out loud, which is exactly why it's worth stating.

Here's the test: if you can insert "_because_" between your data and your claim and the sentence still doesn't make sense without one more idea, that missing idea is your warrant.

**Example:**

- Claim: _"The new library hours will hurt working students."_
- Data: _"The library now closes at 6pm, before most working students get off their evening shifts."_
- Warrant: _"Students who cannot physically get to the library during its open hours cannot use its resources."_

Notice the warrant isn't a new fact, it's the _reasoning move_ that makes the data relevant to the claim at all. Someone could accept the data (library closes at 6pm) and still reject the claim, if they rejected the warrant (maybe they'd argue students can use the library's resources online). That's what makes the warrant the most vulnerable, and most important, part of any argument to identify, whether you're analyzing someone else's or building your own.

**Instructions:**

1. Choose one paragraph from _Common Sense_ or one grievance from the Declaration.
2. In one sentence each, identify its claim, its data, and its warrant.
3. Then write your own original paragraph (any topic) with a clear claim, data, and warrant. Underline each.
