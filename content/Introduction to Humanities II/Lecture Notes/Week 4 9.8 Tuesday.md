## Key Outcomes

Professor Modestino provided a foundational lecture on Aristotle's theories of justice (dike) and virtue (arete) to prepare students for reading Aeschylus's Oresteia 12. The session explored the tension between natural law and positive law, the transition from retributive violence to civic justice, and the mythological background of the House of Atreus 34.

## Concepts of Justice (Dike)

- Evolution of Meaning: Originally, dike referred to "the way things are" or established customs, often manifesting as the rule of kings or the whims of gods 56.
- Talion (Lex Talionis): An early "eye-for-an-eye" system of reciprocal harm, typically restricted to those of equal social rank 78.
- Positive Justice: By the time of Aristotle and Aeschylus, dike evolved into a positive virtue involving institutions, law codes, and courts that bind a community of equals 3.

## Virtue and Character (Arete)

- Definition: Arete is translated as "excellence" or the "perfect fulfillment of a function" rather than the modern Christian sense of virtue 9.
- Cultivation: Virtue is developed through habitual practice (ethos) and the development of character (hexis); for example, one becomes just by doing just things 1011.
- The Golden Mean: Every virtue is a middle ground between two vices (e.g., bravery is the mean between cowardice and recklessness) and is dependent on the specific situation 12.

## Natural Law vs. Positive Law

- General Justice: Defined as obedience to the law (nomos), which serves as a mentorship in virtue 1314.
- Particular Justice: Divided into two types 15:
    
    - Distributive Justice: Allocating goods based on agreed-upon merits (e.g., wealth, virtue, or freedom) 1516.
    - Corrective Justice: Maintaining equality in transactions and harms through balance and reciprocity 1718.
- The Aporia: Aristotle leaves an unresolved contradiction (aporia) regarding whether justice is natural and universal (unchangeable) or positive and legal (custom-based) 141920.

## Mythological Context: The Oresteia

- The House of Atreus: A family line cursed by a cycle of "devouring children," starting with Tantalus and continuing with Atreus 2122.
- Agamemnon's Crime: To sail to Troy, Agamemnon sacrificed his daughter, Iphigenia, repeating the family's pattern of child murder 2324.
- The Furies (Erinyes): Chthonic deities who embody the repressed demand for blood-for-blood retribution that positive law often fails to fully satisfy 2526.
- Clytemnestra's Vendetta: Her murder of Agamemnon is framed as a rebellion against patriarchy, as a wife's rage against a husband was not a recognized "equal" vendetta in Bronze Age customs 2728.
