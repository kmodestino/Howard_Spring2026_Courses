## Key Outcomes

The classes analyzed the opening scenes of Aeschylus’s _Agamemnon_, focusing on the **ritual nature of Greek tragedy**, the **cycle of violence** within the House of Atreus, and the difficulty of interpreting divine signs. Discussion also addressed sustained attention as a core humanities skill, fear, gender-based power dynamics, justice, moral responsibility, and the tension between divine law (**Nomos**) and human action. 123

## Greek Theater Context

- **Ritual and Religion:** Greek tragedy was part of a religious and social ritual performed during the **Festival of Dionysus**, associated with wine, intoxication, revelry, and other forms of heightened experience.
    
- **Performance Structure:** A playwright typically presented three tragedies followed by a satyr play during a single day of the festival. Aeschylus’s _Oresteia_—_Agamemnon_, _The Libation Bearers_, and _The Eumenides_—was performed as a connected trilogy.
    
- **Staging:** Performances took place in large outdoor theaters during daylight. Actors wore masks to communicate character and emotion across the theater, while the chorus served as an intermediary between the play and the audience.
    
- **Historical Perspective:** Athenian audiences understood tragic dramas as representations of a distant heroic age, when gods and heroes were more directly connected to human life. The plays therefore opened a window onto a sacred and troubled past.
    
- **Interpretive Practice:** Close reading requires sustained attention to the language of the text, including imagery, repetition, line structure, and ambiguity. The class emphasized that interpretation is developed through practice rather than treated as a fixed set of facts.
    

## Analysis of the Watchman’s Speech

- **Foreshadowing:** The Watchman’s anxiety, exhaustion, and inability to sleep establish that something is wrong before Agamemnon returns. The personification of the palace—“The house itself, could it take voice”—suggests a curse, haunting, or accumulated history of violence within the House of Atreus.
    
- **Fear and Silence:** The Watchman celebrates the beacon announcing Troy’s fall but refuses to speak openly about what he knows. The image of “an ox” standing on his tongue represents the weight of secrets and the danger of speaking against those in power.
    
- **Gender Dynamics:** Clytemnestra is associated with “male strength of heart,” recognizing her authority while also suggesting that her power is considered unconventional because she is a woman. The title “Agamemnon’s queen” diminishes her authority by defining her through her husband.
    
- **Animal Imagery:** The Watchman describes himself as “dog-wise” on the roof, suggesting obedience, dehumanization, and humiliation. The dog and ox imagery introduces a pattern of animal symbolism that continues throughout the play.
    
- **The Beacon:** The beacon initially represents hope, relief, and victory, but its fire also signifies Troy’s destruction and anticipates the violence that will spread through the House of Atreus. Its meaning changes depending on who interprets it.
    
- **Chance and Instability:** The Watchman’s image of his lord’s dice producing “tripled sixes” presents the beacon as a fortunate throw, but dice also imply uncertainty. Current success does not guarantee future safety.
    

## The Chorus and the Trojan War

- **Role of the Chorus:** The chorus represents the old men of Argos who remained at home while the younger men went to war. The chorus connects the mythical world of the play to the audience, provides moral commentary, and voices truths that other characters avoid expressing.
    
- **The Law of Xenia:** Paris violated **Xenia**, the law of hospitality, by taking Helen after being received as a guest in Menelaus’s household. The violation of this divine law becomes a cause of the Trojan War.
    
- **War and Destruction:** The chorus does not present the Trojan victory as purely celebratory. The war brings death, family separation, destruction, and the decline of the heroic age.
    
- **Repeated Violence:** The relay of beacon fires can be interpreted as an image of violence being passed from one person or generation to another. Each act appears to resolve an earlier conflict while creating the conditions for further revenge.
    

## The Cycle of Violence and Divine Signs

- **Animal Omens:** The eagles tearing apart a pregnant hare echo the history of violence in the House of Atreus, including the killing and consumption of children by an earlier generation.
    
- **The Sacrifice of Iphigenia:** The Greek fleet was unable to sail because of a lack of wind. Calchas interpreted the omen of the eagles and hare as evidence that Artemis demanded the sacrifice of Agamemnon’s daughter, Iphigenia.
    
- **Misinterpretation:** The sacrifice is presented as a possible misreading of the divine sign rather than an unquestionable divine command. Agamemnon’s desire to pursue the war may have encouraged him to accept the interpretation that served his purposes.
    
- **Moral Responsibility:** Agamemnon recognizes that sacrificing Iphigenia is horrifying but chooses to proceed when he treats the war as necessary. The class connected this decision to the distinction between voluntary, involuntary, and mixed actions: difficult circumstances may explain a choice without removing responsibility for it.
    
- **Inevitability of Revenge:** The chorus’s reference to “the secret anger” that remembers the child foreshadows the revenge that will follow Iphigenia’s death. The sacrifice does not restore justice; it creates another blood debt.
    

## Justice, Necessity, and Revenge

- **Justice Through Suffering:** The chorus suggests that people learn justice through suffering. This differs from Aristotle’s account of justice as something developed through practicing just actions.
    
- **Nomos and Divine Law:** Agamemnon’s decision raises questions about whether obedience to divine law or perceived necessity can justify an act that is morally wrong.
    
- **The Cost of Victory:** The repeated refrain about sorrow and eventual good creates deep irony. The Greek victory is accompanied by the death of Iphigenia, the destruction of Troy, the suffering of families, and the continuation of revenge.
    
- **Endless Retaliation:** The play asks whether violence can ever produce stable justice. Attempts to solve one wrong through revenge or sacrifice repeatedly create new wrongs.
